The best sewing machine for quilting is the one that gives you enough room to the right of the needle, feeds several layers evenly and lets you drop the feed dogs (the small toothed bars under the fabric) when you want to quilt freely. For most home quilters who also sew clothes and home décor, the Brother Quilt Club BQ2500 is the strongest all-round pick here, and the Brother CS7000X is the budget pick.

## How we chose these machines

We began with models that appear in quilting roundups, dealer quilting pages and the makers' own quilting ranges, then opened each maker's current US page for the exact model. A machine needed a verified type, a stitch count (or a note that it sews straight stitch only), a buttonhole description where it applies, and a US warranty to be included. Machines that the maker's page showed as discontinued or out of stock, such as the Brother BQ1350 and the Singer Quantum Stylist 9960, were dropped, and so were machines where we could not find a warranty or a second source for a missing figure. Brands whose sites blocked our research client, including Janome and Bernina, are absent for that reason rather than because they are poor choices.

This is a research-based guide. We have not sewn on these machines, and we did not gather enough consistent owner reports to describe how any of them wear over the years, so you will not find claims about long-term reliability here. Where Brother, Baby Lock or Juki give a figure, we cite it; where they give none, we leave it out. Our full approach is on [how we review](/how-we-review/).

The order follows how well each machine's checked features fit a home quilter: space to the right of the needle, feed system, speed control, quilting accessories and warranty. It does not follow what pays us. The site earns a commission on Amazon.com purchases made through links here, and several of these machines (the Baby Lock, for one, is sold through authorized dealers) may earn nothing at all. Near-equals were never reordered for commission.

One term to know before you read on. "Throat space" and "workspace" both mean the open area between the needle and the body of the machine, to the right of the needle. It is where the rolled-up quilt sits while you sew. Makers measure it slightly differently, so treat the figures as a guide for comparing sizes, not a promise about the biggest quilt you can fit.

## What to look for in a sewing machine for quilting

A quilting machine is mostly a sewing machine that is kind to bulk. The things that matter are different from the things on a sewing-machine box, so it helps to know which numbers deserve your attention and which are marketing.

**Space to the right of the needle.** This is the first number to check, and it is the one competitors most often leave vague. A quilt is rolled or folded and pushed through the gap between the needle and the body of the machine. The more open space, the less the quilt drags and bunches. Among the machines here, Baby Lock lists 12 inches <Cite id="C0151" /> and Juki lists 12 inches <Cite id="C0181" /> at the top, Brother lists 11.25 inches <Cite id="C0125" />, and Singer lists 6.25 inches <Cite id="C0028" /> at the bottom. A table that extends the flat surface helps too, and the Juki straight-stitch models list auxiliary tables of 23 x 13 inches <Cite id="C0164" /> and 23 x 14 inches <Cite id="C0174" />. Space matters most for bed-size quilts. For table runners and baby quilts, a smaller workspace is workable.

**How the layers are fed.** A quilt sandwich is three layers: top, batting and backing. A standard presser foot grips only the top layer, so the bottom layer can creep and leave puckers. A walking foot or a built-in dual feed fixes this by moving the top layer along with the bottom. The Brother BQ2500 includes a dual feed foot <Cite id="C0129" />, Juki lists JUKI Smart Feed on the NX7 <Cite id="C0184" />, the Brother CS7000X lists a walking foot in the box <Cite id="C0148" />, and the Baby Lock lists a walking foot as an optional accessory <Cite id="C0196" />. If your machine lacks one, buy a walking foot made for the exact model, because feet are not interchangeable across brands.

**Drop feed for free-motion quilting.** Feed dogs are the small toothed bars that pull fabric along under the needle. For free-motion quilting, where you guide the fabric by hand to draw stitched patterns, you lower them out of the way. The Juki models list a switch that drops the feed dogs <Cite id="C0166" />, the Baby Lock lists a drop feed <Cite id="C0158" /> and the Brother BQ950 lists a manual drop feed <Cite id="C0122" />. If you hope to quilt freely, confirm the drop feed before anything else.

**Speed control.** Top speed is a maximum, not a typical pace. What helps is control: the ability to run slowly and steadily. The Juki TL-2010Q lists a speed lever that sets any speed from 200 to 1,500 stitches per minute <Cite id="C0167" />. The Singer 4423 lists a top speed of 1,100 stitches per minute <Cite id="C0027" />, which suits experienced hands better than someone sewing their first quilt.

**Needle up/down and a knee lift.** Needle up/down stops the needle in the fabric, so you can pivot at corners without losing your place. A knee lift raises the presser foot without a hand, which keeps both hands on the quilt. Several machines here list one or both, such as the Brother BQ950 <Cite id="C0120" /> and the Baby Lock Jazz II <Cite id="C0155" />.

**Thread cutter and needle threader.** These do not change stitch quality, but they save time. Quilting means a lot of starts and stops, and an automatic thread cutter, such as the one listed for the Brother BQ2500 <Cite id="C0135" />, spares a pair of scissors each time. A needle threader helps most when your eyes tire. The Juki TL-2010Q lists one <Cite id="C0165" />.

**Built-in stitch count.** Ignore the headline number if you mostly quilt. The Baby Lock Jazz II lists 28 stitches <Cite id="C0149" /> and the Brother BQ2500 lists 729 <Cite id="C0123" />, yet both can piece the same quilt. A long stitch list only matters if you also sew garments, home décor or decorative quilting, and it comes with a screen and menus to learn.

## Straight-stitch machine or an all-rounder?

The biggest choice in this guide is not Brother against Juki. It is whether you want a machine that does one job or many.

A straight-stitch machine, such as the Juki TL-2010Q <Cite id="C0160" /> or the TL-2000Qi <Cite id="C0171" />, sews a single stitch type and nothing else. That sounds limiting, but piecing a quilt top, quilting straight lines and free-motion quilting mostly use a straight stitch. With one stitch to manage, there is less to set up and the machine can focus on feeding and speed control. Both Juki models list a top speed of 1,500 stitches per minute <Cite id="C0161" />, <Cite id="C0172" />. The price is range: no zigzag, no buttonholes, no stretch stitches, so a hem on a knit or a buttonhole on a shirt needs another machine.

An all-rounder, such as the Brother BQ2500 or the Juki HZL-NX7, sews quilts and also handles garments, mending and home décor. It lists far more stitches and buttonholes, and it usually adds a touchscreen, a thread cutter and dual feed. The trade is more menus, more weight and often a higher price. If you only own one machine and sew more than quilts, an all-rounder is the safer choice.

If you quilt often and already own a basic sewing machine, a straight-stitch Juki or the simple Baby Lock Jazz II is worth a look. If you are starting out and want one machine for everything, the CS7000X or the BQ2500 is a better start. And if you are a beginner quilter weighing price against room to grow, our [guide to the best sewing machines for beginners](/best-sewing-machine-for-beginners/) covers the beginner-friendly side without the quilting focus.

**Sewing and quilting plus embroidery.** Some quilters also want embroidery in the same machine, to add names, labels or motifs to quilts. That is a different category with its own costs, because embroidery needs a hoop, designs and stabilizer. Our [embroidery machine guide](/best-embroidery-machine/) covers combination machines, and none of the machines in this guide embroider.

## The best sewing machine for a beginner quilter

If you are new to quilting, the best machine is rarely the biggest or the most expensive. It is the one that lets you finish a first quilt without fighting it. Two machines in this guide suit that job in different ways.

The Brother CS7000X is the cheaper route. It sits in the $200–$300 band and lists a walking foot, a quilting foot, a quilt guide and a wide table in the box <Cite id="C0148" />, which is most of what a first quilt needs. Its top speed of 750 stitches per minute <Cite id="C0147" /> is the lowest in this guide, which is no handicap while you are learning to keep seams straight. The catch is that Brother gives no workspace figure for it, so make your first projects small: a table runner, a baby quilt or a set of placemats.

The Brother BQ950 is the step up if you already know quilting will stick. It is sold as a quilting model and lists 15 included feet, among them a walking foot and a 1/4 inch quilting foot with guide <Cite id="C0118" />. Its workspace is 4.1 x 8.3 inches <Cite id="C0116" />, still modest, but it adds a knee lifter, a thread cutter and needle up/down <Cite id="C0120" /> that make a long afternoon of piecing less tiring.

A third route is the Baby Lock Jazz II, which has few stitches but lists 12 inches to the right of the needle <Cite id="C0151" /> and sells through dealers who can teach you the basics. It is worth a look if there is a Baby Lock dealer near you and you would rather have a person to ask.

Whatever you choose, plan on three habits that matter more than the machine. Start each project with a fresh needle. Use a thread you trust. And practice on a small sample before you sew the real thing. If you would like a wider view of entry-level choices, our [beginner sewing machine guide](/best-sewing-machine-for-beginners/) covers machines that are easy to learn but not built around quilting.

## Which machine for which kind of quilter

A quick way to choose is to start with the quilting you actually do, then work back to the machine.

**You piece tops and send them to a longarm.** You need a machine that sews accurate, steady seams, and the workspace matters less because you are not rolling a finished quilt under the arm. A Juki straight-stitch model, the TL-2010Q or the lower-cost TL-2000Qi, is built around that job. Both list a top speed of 1,500 stitches per minute <Cite id="C0161" />, <Cite id="C0172" /> and the TL-2010Q adds a speed lever that lets you keep the pace slow while you learn <Cite id="C0167" />.

**You quilt your own bed-size quilts on a home machine.** Workspace and feeding come first. The Brother BQ2500 <Cite id="C0125" /> and the Juki HZL-NX7 <Cite id="C0181" /> list the most room among the computerized machines here, and both include a dual feed system. If you prefer fewer menus, the Baby Lock Jazz II lists 12 inches <Cite id="C0151" /> with a dial and a knee lift.

**You make smaller quilts, wall hangings and gifts.** You can save money. The CS7000X and the Singer 4423 both sit in the $200–$300 band, and the Brother BQ950 gives you quilting feet at a step up.

**You sew garments, bags and home décor as well.** An all-rounder makes sense. The BQ2500, BQ3100, NX7 and F600 list stitch counts from 225 <Cite id="C0188" /> to 729 <Cite id="C0123" />, plus buttonhole styles.

**You want to carry your machine to classes.** Weight matters. The Juki HZL-F600 lists 21.6 lb <Cite id="C0191" />, the straight-stitch Jukis list 25.4 lb <Cite id="C0163" />, and the Baby Lock Jazz II lists 27.5 lb <Cite id="C0153" />. The BQ2500 and BQ3100 list about 34.4 lb <Cite id="C0127" />.

Notice what is missing from the list: the machine with the longest stitch list. For quilting, it almost never decides the outcome.

## Home machine or longarm?

A home sewing machine pieces a quilt top and can quilt the layers by pushing a rolled quilt under the arm. A longarm is a different machine: a long frame that holds the whole quilt while the sewing head moves over it. Longarms suit people who quilt many large quilts or sell their work, and they cost several times as much as any machine here and need a dedicated room.

For most home quilters, a good domestic machine with a large workspace is plenty. Many people piece at home and send finished tops to a longarm service. That is a good fit if you love piecing but not wrestling a king-size quilt. Our [quilting machines hub](/quilting-machines/) gathers the pieces on both options, and the longarm guides will be linked here when they are published.

## Warranty, service and support in the US

Warranty terms differ more than the headline numbers suggest. Brother lists a 2/6/25 year premium limited warranty on the Quilt Club machines <Cite id="C0134" />, meaning different parts are covered for different periods rather than everything for 25 years. The CS7000X lists a 25-year limited warranty with free phone and online support for the life of the product <Cite id="C0005" />. Singer lists up to 25 years of limited coverage with some restrictions <Cite id="C0030" />.

Baby Lock lists a 25-year limited warranty, but the breakdown is 10 years on parts, 2 on electrical, 1 on circuit boards and 1 on labor <Cite id="C0157" />. Juki America lists 2 years on motors and electronics and 5 years on other parts <Cite id="C0169" />. Juki's terms also say the warranty is for the original purchaser, for household use, and is valid only in the United States and Canada.

**Where service comes from.** This is the part that decides how a warranty feels in practice. Baby Lock says its machines are sold only through authorized retailers, who handle warranty claims and yearly service <Cite id="C0159" />. Juki's warranty asks you to deliver the machine to the dealer you bought it from, or to an address Juki designates, and to keep your receipt. Juki's terms also say periodic servicing by an authorized dealer is part of keeping the warranty valid, so ask a dealer what that means locally.

If you live far from a dealer, check whether a service center is within a drive before buying a machine that is repaired only by dealers. For machines bought online, the maker's support line is often your first contact, and shipping a heavy machine for repair is a cost to factor in.

## Where to buy and availability

You have two routes: an authorized dealer or a large online store. A dealer is the usual route for the Baby Lock and Juki machines. It gives you a person to ask, a chance to sit at the machine with a rolled quilt, and local service. A dealer may also offer classes or a maintenance plan. Prices at dealers can be higher or lower than online, and many dealers match or discount, so ask.

Online stores, including Amazon.com for the models that are sold there, are convenient and often competitive on price for the Brother and Singer machines, but you will normally deal with the maker's support for problems. Availability changes: during our research, Brother's page showed the BQ1350 as discontinued and Singer's page showed the 9960 as out of stock, so check that the exact model you want is current. Avoid look-alike model numbers and retailer-exclusive bundles, which are different machines with different specs.

A short checklist before you click buy: confirm the exact model, check what is in the box, confirm the warranty applies in the US, and find out who services it near you.

## Setting up any machine for better quilting

A few low-cost choices make any of these machines sew a quilt more smoothly, and they matter as much as the model you pick.

**Needles and thread.** Quilting runs through several layers, so a sharp needle makes a difference. A pack of <AffiliateLink query="quilting machine needles">quilting needles</AffiliateLink> is a small spend, and many quilters swap to a new needle at the start of each quilt. Use a good-quality thread in the top and the bobbin, and test your tension on a scrap made of the same top, batting and backing as your quilt.

**The right foot for the job.** A straight-stitch foot and a straight-stitch needle plate help with fine piecing, which is why Brother adds both to the Quilters Bundle for the BQ950 <Cite id="C0121" />. A walking foot helps when you quilt through layers. A free-motion foot is for drawing patterns with the feed dogs lowered, as on the Baby Lock <Cite id="C0158" />. Check which feet your model includes, because feet are not interchangeable between brands.

**A flat surface.** Support the quilt so its weight does not pull on the needle. The wide table that comes with the Brother CS7000X <Cite id="C0148" /> and the Juki HZL-NX7 <Cite id="C0186" /> helps, and so does an extension table for the Baby Lock <Cite id="C0156" />. Even a clear space on your desk or a folding table beside the machine keeps the quilt from dragging.

**Cleaning and servicing.** Quilting makes a lot of lint. Brush it from around the feed dogs and the bobbin area regularly, and have the machine serviced by an authorized dealer on the schedule in your manual. Juki's warranty terms list periodic dealer servicing as a condition for coverage, so keep your receipts.

**Take your time.** The machine does not do the quilting for you. A steady, slow speed and a rested pair of hands give better results than any extra feature, and most of the machines here give you a speed control for exactly that reason.

## Common mistakes, and who should look elsewhere

**Buying on stitch count.** The Jazz II lists 28 stitches <Cite id="C0149" /> and still suits quilting well. If you only piece and quilt, put the money into workspace and feeding instead.

**Forgetting the workspace.** A machine can sew beautifully and still be miserable with a big quilt. If you plan bed quilts, favor a verified workspace of 11 to 12 inches, as listed for the BQ2500 <Cite id="C0125" /> and the Jazz II <Cite id="C0151" />, over small-quilt machines such as the Singer 4423 <Cite id="C0028" />.

**Skipping the walking foot.** Layers shift without one. Check that your machine includes a walking foot or dual feed, or budget for one.

**Expecting one machine to do everything.** Straight-stitch machines are fast and focused, but they cannot sew buttonholes <Cite id="C0160" />. Decide first whether you need that.

**Ignoring weight and where it lives.** The BQ2500 and BQ3100 list about 34.4 lb <Cite id="C0127" />, <Cite id="C0140" />. If you move your machine often, a lighter model such as the Juki HZL-F600 at 21.6 lb <Cite id="C0191" /> is easier to carry.

**Who should look elsewhere.** If you want a longarm or a sit-down frame for large quilts, none of these is the right tool, and our [quilting machines hub](/quilting-machines/) is the place to start. If you mostly want to embroider, see the [embroidery machine guide](/best-embroidery-machine/). If you are brand new to sewing and unsure whether quilting will stick, a simpler beginner machine may be enough, as in our [beginner sewing machine guide](/best-sewing-machine-for-beginners/).
