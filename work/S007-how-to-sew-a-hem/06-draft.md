To sew a hem, fold the raw edge of the fabric up to the wrong side, press the fold flat, and stitch it down close to the fold's edge. A hem is a folded and sewn edge that stops fabric from unraveling, and it also sets the length of a garment <Cite id="C0197" />. Done on a sewing machine, a basic hem takes a few minutes once you have pressed it well.

This guide shows how to sew a hem with a sewing machine, how to pick the right hem for your fabric, and how to fix the usual problems. It is built from published sewing guidance, not from our own sewing, and each figure links to its source.

## What you need before you start

- Your fabric, already cut and any side seams sewn.
- A sewing machine with a straight stitch and a zigzag stitch.
- An iron and ironing board. Pressing does more for a neat hem than any stitch setting.
- Pins or clips, and a ruler or seam gauge for even folds.
- Thread that matches the fabric, and a needle suited to it. Needle makers publish charts: Schmetz lists Universal needles in sizes 70/10 to 90/14 for woven cotton <Cite id="C0208" />, and Jersey needles in 70/10 to 90/14 (or Stretch 75/11 and 90/14) for cotton knits <Cite id="C0209" />.

<Callout type="tip">
Before you stitch the real garment, sew a test hem on a scrap of the same fabric. Brother's manual recommends a trial sewing with fabric and thread that match the project <Cite id="C0207" />.
</Callout>

If the needle choice feels confusing, the <a href="/tools/needle-thread-selector/">Needle &amp; Thread Selector</a> gives a starting point by fabric.

## How to sew a double-fold hem, step by step

A double-fold hem turns the raw edge under once, then folds the hem up a second time, so no raw edge shows inside. It suits most woven fabrics such as cotton, linen and light-to-medium fabrics. One university extension guide describes this "narrow double" method for wovens, which we follow below <Cite id="C0199" />.

<Steps>
<Step title="Trim and mark the hem">
Try the garment on or measure the length you want, then mark the hemline. Trim the hem allowance (the spare fabric below the hemline) to 5/8 inch for a woven fabric <Cite id="C0199" />. Allowances vary by garment, so a skirt may use more; follow your pattern.
</Step>
<Step title="Turn the raw edge under and press">
Fold the raw edge under 1/8 inch toward the wrong side and press it <Cite id="C0199" />. Use the iron's heat setting that suits your fabric and press, rather than slide, so the fold keeps its shape.
</Step>
<Step title="Fold up on the hemline and press again">
Fold the hem up along the marked hemline, so the turned-under edge sits inside. Press, then pin or clip every few inches with the pins pointing across the fold.
</Step>
<Step title="Stitch near the folded edge">
Set the machine to a straight stitch and sew close to the first fold, on the wrong side or right side as your pattern prefers. The extension guide then adds a second, parallel row that catches the upper edge of the hem <Cite id="C0199" />. Use the edge of the presser foot as a guide and keep your eyes on the fold, not the needle.
</Step>
<Step title="Press the finished hem">
Give the hem a final press from the wrong side. This sets the stitches and flattens the fold.
</Step>
</Steps>

A narrow, thin hem like this works on light fabrics. For a heavier fabric or a deeper look, the same extension guide describes a topstitched hem with a 1 1/4 inch allowance, turned under 1/4 inch, with a second row of stitching 1/4 inch below the first <Cite id="C0201" />.

## Which kind of hem should you sew?

The right hem depends on the fabric and how visible the stitching should be.

- **Double-fold (or narrow double) hem.** The everyday choice for woven fabrics. Neat inside, simple to sew.
- **Topstitched hem.** Shows a line of stitching on the outside, which can look deliberate and sturdy on casual clothes and home items.
- **Zigzag hem.** Better for stretchy knits, because the stitch gives with the fabric.
- **Blind hem.** Stitches that are nearly invisible on the right side, often used on skirts, dresses and trousers. It takes practice, and Singer's help page says to make a test first <Cite id="C0205" />.
- **Rolled hem.** A very narrow hem for thin fabrics, often sewn with a special foot.

Reference sources also name overlocked and folded, bias tape and rolled hems among the common types <Cite id="C0198" />. This page focuses on the basic machine hem.

## Hemming stretchy knits such as T-shirts

Knit fabric stretches, so a plain straight stitch can snap when the hem is pulled. Choose a stitch that stretches too.

For stretchable knits like jersey, the extension guide suggests trimming the allowance to 5/8 inch, pressing on the hemline, and stitching with a narrow, close zigzag about 1/8 inch wide <Cite id="C0202" />. For topstitched knit hems it advises a stitch length slightly longer than regular stitching <Cite id="C0203" />. The extension guide also trims a knit hem to 1/2 inch in its narrow double method <Cite id="C0200" />.

Use a needle made for knits. Schmetz lists Jersey needles in 70/10 to 90/14, or Stretch 75/11 and 90/14, for cotton knits <Cite id="C0209" />. A new needle can matter here: a worn one is more likely to skip stitches on stretchy fabric. Sewing a T-shirt or shirt hem follows the same steps, just with the zigzag or stretch setting and gentle handling so the fabric does not stretch as it feeds.

## Common mistakes and how to fix them

**Wavy or stretched hem.** Usually the fabric was pulled while sewing, or the hem was not pressed first. Let the feed dogs (the small teeth under the foot) move the fabric, and steer lightly. On knits, a zigzag stitch and a damp pressing cloth help flatten ripples <Cite id="C0202" />.

**Skipped stitches or puckers.** Check the needle first. A dull needle or the wrong type is a frequent cause. For heavy fabric such as denim, Schmetz lists Jeans needles in 90/14 to 110/18 <Cite id="C0210" />.

**Skipping the test.** Different fabrics behave differently, so a scrap test saves a ruined garment <Cite id="C0207" />.

**Raw edge still fraying inside.** On medium to heavy fabrics, finish the raw edge with an overcast or zigzag stitch before folding. Singer's blind hem page makes this point: turn the edge under on fine fabrics, or overcast it on medium to heavyweight ones <Cite id="C0206" />.

**Uneven fold depth.** Use a ruler or seam gauge and press each fold before stitching, not after.

## What this means for choosing a machine

You do not need an expensive machine to hem well. A straight stitch and a zigzag are enough for the methods above. Some machines add a blind hem stitch, and extension guidance lists the blind hem foot and rolled hem foot as feet that can cut down on sewing time <Cite id="C0204" />. A free arm (the narrow sewing bed that slides off) makes hemming sleeves and trouser legs easier.
